# Kaggriculture 1st Place Solution: Iterating Between Behavior Cloning and Self-Play PPO

We finished 1st out of 10,148 teams in Kaggriculture. We initialized our policy with behavior cloning (BC) on public replays and then improved it through self-play PPO. Throughout training, whenever self-play exposed gaps in the agent's strategy, we introduced targeted demonstrations from public replays or our heuristic solver and resumed PPO to refine the resulting behavior.

Both final submissions used Transformers with approximately 10.2 million parameters. Across their respective model lineages, the self-play transition counts correspond to approximately 8.29 million games for Final A and 9.04 million for Final B, counting 1,438 transitions per game (719 per player). We also experimented with larger models, but selected the roughly 10M models for our final submissions and added rules to improve inventory management and resolve conflicting actions.

Our solution differed from those of other teams in four main ways: (1) iteratively introducing missing strategies through short BC phases using public replays from other teams and games generated by our own heuristic solver, then resuming PPO; (2) estimating the opponent's hidden inventory from public information; (3) applying 2D RoPE only within the same farm; (4) incorporating inference-time correction rules into PPO rollouts through action masking.

Our code is available on [GitHub](https://github.com/msdsm/kaggriculture-1st-solution).

## Solution Overview

The table below summarizes our final models, training setup, and submission agents. We use **Unit** to refer to the farmer and hired workers, and **Market** to refer to market orders.

| Component | Our approach |
|---|---|
| Observations | Both players' farms and Units, market information, global game state, and opponent inventory estimated from public information |
| Model | Shared Transformer with approximately 10.2M parameters, 12 layers, width 256, 8 attention heads, and GELU activations |
| Action outputs | 500 candidates per Unit and 1,903 candidates for each of 10 Market slots, computed in parallel |
| Initialization | Imitation learning from multiple public policies, followed by pretraining the value head to predict the game outcome |
| Continued training | Alternating self-play PPO with additional imitation learning to introduce new strategies |
| Reward | Terminal reward of +1 for a win, 0 for a draw, and −1 for a loss; no intermediate rewards |
| Infrastructure | Rust environments on CPUs and distributed JAX training on GPUs |
| Final submissions | One 10M model with inference-time corrections, and another trained with those correction rules incorporated into action selection |

In each iteration, replay analysis helped us identify missing strategies, the heuristic solver supplied demonstrations for situations the neural network (NN) struggled with, and PPO refined the newly introduced behavior to improve win rates. Our 10M and 20M neural networks and our standalone heuristic solver each reached 1st place on the public leaderboard at different points during the competition.

## Behavior Cloning and PPO

We used initial imitation learning to establish a starting policy for self-play, pretrained the value head, and then improved the agent with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). Later in training, we repeated this process with short BC phases to introduce missing strategies. We evaluated BC by the behaviors it made available for subsequent self-play, as well as by the agent's strength immediately after imitation learning.

### Building the Initial Policy from Multiple Teachers

The long sequence of actions before receiving a win/loss reward made learning from a random policy look difficult, so we initialized the policy with BC.

For the initial BC phase, we pooled replays from 34 submissions and split the data into training and validation sets at the game level. We trained for 30 epochs on the sum of the Unit and Market cross-entropy losses, without entropy regularization or a KL penalty to a reference policy at this stage. We selected teachers for both their current strength and their strategic diversity: imitating only one style of farm management seemed likely to limit the alternatives that PPO would explore later.

The model trained on the broader teacher pool was weaker immediately after BC. Against an early submission of our own, the model trained on all 34 submissions scored 10 wins and 90 losses over 100 games, using seeds 0–49 from both player seats. A separate model trained on a narrower teacher pool scored 82 wins and 18 losses against the same opponent. We nevertheless chose the model trained on the broader teacher pool as our starting point for exploration.

These experiments included neither reinforcement learning (RL) from random initialization nor matched PPO runs from the two BC initializations, so they do not establish a causal benefit from teacher diversity.

### Teaching New Strategies Through Imitation Learning

During self-play, the agent sometimes failed to learn how to use the fourth land plot, or stopped using particular crops and livestock. Making the fourth plot productive requires a coordinated sequence: buying the land, hiring enough workers, planting, harvesting, and selling. PPO alone struggled to discover this sequence from a policy that did not use the plot at all.

Tomatoes presented a similar problem. Under typical shop configurations, they were less profitable than strawberries, but they could become valuable when several pizza shops or greengrocers appeared. Once the policy learned to avoid tomatoes in typical games, it could also lose the ability to grow them effectively when conditions favored them.

We addressed these gaps by adding targeted demonstrations and inserting short BC phases. At one stage in the 10M model lineage, we added 849 games from seven submissions and 300 games of solver self-play. Replay analysis guided the choice of data: public policies demonstrated how to use the fourth plot, while the solver demonstrated how to adapt production to price spikes for particular products. We describe the solver's search procedure in [Heuristic Solver](#heuristic-solver).

Each additional BC phase lasted 1–3 epochs, followed by value pretraining and a return to PPO. The loss included cross-entropy and entropy terms. We also tried a KL penalty using a frozen copy of the policy from before the additional BC phase. Let $\rho$ denote that frozen policy and $\pi_\theta$ the policy being trained. With Unit and Market cross-entropies $\mathrm{CE}_U,\mathrm{CE}_M$, KL divergences $D_U,D_M$ in the direction $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\Vert\pi_\theta)$, and entropies $H_U,H_M$, the objective with KL regularization was:

$$
L_{\mathrm{BC}}
=\mathrm{CE}_U+\mathrm{CE}_M
+\beta(D_U+D_M)
-\alpha_U H_U-\alpha_M H_M.
$$

The following table compares three regularization settings after two epochs of training on the same additional data. Entropy was me!asured during self-play with each model selecting its highest-probability actions, so the models visited different states. Normalized entropy is $H(p)/\log K$, where $K$ is the number of action candidates.

| Unit entropy coefficient | Market entropy coefficient | KL coefficient | Unit normalized entropy | Market normalized entropy | W–L vs. the pre-BC policy |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.005 | 0.005 | 0 | 0.0437 | 0.0330 | 3–97 |
| 0.02 | 0.05 | 0.01 | 0.0687 | 0.0520 | 0–100 |
| 0.10 | 0.10 | 0.05 | 0.1271 | 0.1168 | 0–100 |

We chose the third configuration despite its 0–100 record immediately after BC because it kept both normalized entropies near our practical target of 0.1. For comparison, before this BC phase, the Unit and Market entropies were 0.1292 and 0.3715, respectively. After further PPO training, the resulting agent surpassed the pre-BC policy.

We saw a similar pattern in the 20M model lineage. After additional BC from public policies that used the fourth plot, the agent began not only buying the fourth plot but also planting and harvesting crops there. It sometimes lost to the original model immediately afterward, but after resuming PPO, it improved while continuing to use the fourth plot. Separately evaluating the introduction of new behaviors and their eventual contribution to winning helped us continue this training cycle.

### PPO Setup

We optimized only the terminal outcome: +1 for a win, 0 for a draw, and −1 for a loss. We did not use intermediate rewards for bank balance or harvest volume. The main settings were:

| Parameter | Setting |
|---|---|
| Discount factor $\gamma$ | 1 |
| Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) $\lambda$ | 0.97 |
| PPO clipping parameter | 0.2 |
| Value loss | Huber loss, generally with a coefficient of 2 during PPO |
| Advantage normalization | Mean and variance computed over all valid samples across all GPUs in an update |
| Reference policy update | Generally, replace the reference when a candidate wins at least 70 of 100 games against it |

We collected complete games and computed GAE and value targets for each player as follows. Here, $T$ is the number of transitions, $r_t$ is the reward, $V_t$ is that player's value estimate, and the terminal value is $V_T=0$.

$$
\delta_t=r_t+\gamma V_{t+1}-V_t,\qquad
A_t=\sum_{\ell=0}^{T-1-t}(\gamma\lambda)^\ell\delta_{t+\ell},
\qquad R_t=A_t+V_t.
$$

A game contains 719 transitions per player. If all value estimates are zero, the terminal reward contributes to the first advantage estimate with a weight of only $0.97^{718}\simeq3\times10^{-10}$. Value estimates at intermediate states are therefore essential for propagating information about the outcome through this long sequence. After BC, we froze the policy and shared Transformer and pretrained the value head on complete games before starting PPO.

The training objective combined the policy loss, value loss, a KL penalty to a reference policy, and separate entropy terms for Unit and Market. We used KL divergence from the reference policy $\rho$ to the policy being trained, $\pi_\theta$:

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\Vert\pi_\theta\mid o)
=\sum_a\rho(a\mid o)
\log\frac{\rho(a\mid o)}{\pi_\theta(a\mid o)}.
$$

This direction strongly penalizes abruptly dropping actions that the reference policy considered likely, while leaving room for the current policy to explore strategies absent from the reference. We held the reference fixed across multiple rollouts and replaced it with a candidate after confirming an improvement in head-to-head games. It served a different purpose from PPO's old policy, which changed with each rollout.

Updating the reference—generally after at least 70 wins in 100 games—prevented the KL term from continually pulling the agent back toward its initial policy. Beating the reference did not imply improvement against every opponent, so we also evaluated against other agents.

We used separate entropy coefficients for Unit and Market and monitored normalized entropy to account for their different numbers of action candidates, $K$:

$$
H(p)=-\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_i\log p_i,\qquad
H_{\mathrm{norm}}(p)=\frac{H(p)}{\log K}.
$$

Immediately after BC, the policy was concentrated around the teachers' actions, and we sometimes initialized a fresh Adam state for PPO. We used learning-rate warmup to avoid abrupt updates. In one run after additional BC, the learning rate increased from zero to $5\times10^{-5}$ over 240 Adam updates. With 24 updates per rollout, this corresponded to 10 rollouts. This was one configuration we used, not an established optimum.

## Infrastructure and Development Workflow

### RL Environment and Hardware

We ported the official game environment and feature extraction to Rust and ran multiple games on CPUs. Observations were passed to Python as contiguous arrays, with batched inference and PPO updates handled by JAX on GPUs. Opponent inventory estimation was also part of the Rust environment's per-turn processing. We validated the port by running identical action sequences in our implementation and the official environment, comparing state transitions, rewards, and observations.

For continued training of the 10M models, we used data parallelism across a mix of A30 and A100 GPUs. Our largest recorded configuration used 48 GPUs—30 A30s and 18 A100s—and achieved approximately 25–26 games per second, including both game collection and PPO updates.

### Working with Coding Agents

For NN development, we delegated implementation, bug fixes, experiment execution, progress monitoring, result logging, and environment optimization to coding agents, without reviewing their code. We chose the architectures and experiment directions ourselves, then used the reported results to decide what to try next. Coding agents were less helpful with architecture and experimental design. For example, preserving diversity during BC for later exploration and producing Unit and Market actions in parallel for speed were our own design decisions.

Although the agents could run the experiments, interpreting strategies still required inspecting the board. In one case, a policy described as competitive with those of the top teams based on its average cash and win rate actually grew only strawberries and used no livestock. In another, a teacher policy was mistakenly assumed to come from a top team simply because it appeared frequently in the dataset.

We therefore required coding-agent reports to include at least one example game, identified by a game ID or illustrated with a board excerpt, before drawing conclusions about a policy. Inspecting the game state and matchup conditions, alongside aggregate metrics, was necessary for selecting teachers and identifying weaknesses.

## Model Architecture

We used an entity Transformer: board cells, Units, products, and other entities were represented as tokens, and a shared Transformer encoder produced Unit actions, Market orders, and value estimates.

![](https://www.googleapis.com/download/storage/v1/b/kaggle-user-content/o/inbox%2F4817149%2F744012e97ebc114e4cd490e468fa3b60%2Faction_heads.png?generation=1793114922247536&alt=media)

### Tokenizing Observations

The input included all 200 cells across both players' farms, both players' Units, market information for each product, and global game information. We estimated the opponent's hidden inventory from public information and included it as a separate token. Each of the 10 Market order slots also had its own token.

| Token type | Main features | Input dimensions |
|---|---|---:|
| Global | Time, bank balances, unlocked land, shops, total inventory, free warehouse capacity, worker count | 34 |
| Cell | Farm identity, coordinates, terrain, crop/livestock state, Unit occupancy | 34 |
| Unit | Farm identity, coordinates, farmer/worker type, carried inventory, warehouse access | 22 |
| Product | Product identity, warehouse inventory, seed inventory, market inventory, price, town demand | 19 |
| Opponent inventory estimate | Estimated total and carried quantities by product, uncertainty, unexplained changes in bank balance, etc. | 50 |
| Market slot | Position among the 10 order slots | 10 |

Each token type had its own linear projection into a shared 256-dimensional space. Categorical features were one-hot encoded; quantities and bank balances were represented as scaled numerical features.

We also supplied aggregate features: total warehouse inventory, free warehouse capacity, the total quantity carried by all Units, and the total carried by each Unit. Given the Transformer's limited depth, we supplied these aggregates to reduce the need for it to learn to sum quantities across many tokens. We retained the individual features and did not run an ablation isolating the effect of the aggregates.

### Transformer and 2D RoPE Within Each Farm

The final model was a pre-LayerNorm Transformer with 12 layers, width 256, and 8 attention heads. Each layer used a feed-forward network (FFN) with an intermediate dimension of 1,024 and GELU activation, with residual connections around both attention and the FFN. We chose this size to balance training throughput with CPU inference at submission time.

For positional encoding, we applied 2D rotary position embeddings (RoPE) only to attention between tokens representing cells or Units on the same farm. The two farms do not form a single traversable plane, so coordinate differences across farms have no spatial meaning. Attention involving Global, Product, and Market slot tokens used ordinary dot products without assigning artificial coordinates to those tokens. Tokens on different farms could still attend to each other.

For one-dimensional coordinates $x_i,x_j$, query and key vectors $q_i,k_j$, and a position-dependent rotation $R(x)$, RoPE introduces relative position into the dot product:

$$
\bigl(R(x_i)q_i\bigr)^\top\bigl(R(x_j)k_j\bigr)
=q_i^\top R(x_j-x_i)k_j.
$$

Of the 32 dimensions in each head, 8 were used for the horizontal coordinate and 8 for the vertical coordinate; the remaining 16 were left unrotated. We used a base of 100. Absolute coordinates remained in the input features, allowing the model to use both the relative positions of cells and Units and absolute locations such as farm boundaries and the warehouse.

### Action Heads

To maximize self-play throughput, we computed action logits for all Units and Market order slots in a single NN forward pass. An autoregressive design would have required a separate NN evaluation for each Unit or order. Instead, we passed each encoder output token through a head shared across tokens of the same type.

The Unit head produced 500 categorical action candidates per Unit. For PICKUP and PLACE, each combination of operation, product, and quantity was a separate category. The candidates were:

- 480 PICKUP/PLACE actions: 2 operations × 12 products × 20 quantities.
- 5 crop-specific PLANT actions.
- 15 other actions, including movement and watering.

For each 256-dimensional Unit representation $h_U$, we computed 
$$\ell_U=W_Uh_U+b_U\in\mathbb{R}^{500}$$ 
and applied softmax to obtain action probabilities.

The Market head produced 1,903 candidates for each of the 10 order slots. Of these, 900 were SELL actions (9 products × 100 quantities), with probabilities factorized into a product distribution and a product-specific quantity distribution over 1–100 units. The other 1,003 candidates consisted of one no-order action, 1,000 purchase actions (five seed types, wheat, fertilizer, and three livestock types, each available in quantities of 1–100), one HIRE action, and one BUY_LAND action.

Parallel outputs can cause multiple Units to compete for the same cell or inventory. Later Market orders also do not directly observe the inventory changes caused by earlier orders. We addressed these issues near the end of the competition with corrections for conflicting and invalid actions.

### Zero-Sum Value Estimates

The value head used the Global token, a 256-dimensional hidden layer, and GELU activation. We added a linear term based on the bank balance difference, then combined both players' outputs at the same game timestep to obtain zero-sum value estimates. This design was inspired by the [Orbit Wars implementation](https://github.com/IsaiahPressman/kaggle-orbit-wars/blob/32b3ec900ad406eedd965f53a1a0f4490d31c589/python/owl/model/stateless_transformer_v1.py#L1494).

Let $o_i$ be the observation for player $i\in\{0,1\}$, $f_\theta(o_i)$ the NN output, $m_i$ that player's bank balance, $m_{1-i}$ the opponent's bank balance, and $\tau$ the fraction of the game elapsed. Before the final transformation, the output was:

$$
z_i=f_\theta(o_i)
+(a\tau+b)\frac{m_i-m_{1-i}}{20{,}000}.
$$

The coefficients $a,b$ were trainable. This gave the model direct access to the balance difference and allowed its influence to vary over the course of the game.

We applied softmax across the two players' outputs and mapped the result to $[-1,1]$:

$$
V_i=2\frac{\exp(z_i)}{\exp(z_0)+\exp(z_1)}-1,
\qquad V_0+V_1=0.
$$

For two players, this is equivalent to $V_0=\tanh((z_0-z_1)/2)$ and $V_1=-V_0$. Each policy selected actions using only its own observation, whereas the value calculation used both players' observations from the training environment. We preserved these player pairs when computing the value loss. Value estimates were not used for action selection in the submitted agent.

### Estimating the Opponent's Hidden Inventory

We wanted the policy to have access to all strategically relevant information. The game hides the contents of the opponent's warehouse and the composition of the inventory carried by their Units. If those inventories were visible, the state would be fully observable, with simultaneous actions remaining the source of uncertainty.

We therefore estimated the opponent's inventory from observable changes between turns, including board updates revealing Unit activity, changes in market inventory and prices, and bank-balance changes. We supplied these estimates as features. Rather than feeding the entire observation history to the NN, we used a state observer to summarize it.

We tracked 12 product types: wheat, carrots, tomatoes, strawberries, melons, eggs, milk, wool, fertilizer, geese, cows, and sheep. The estimates covered items stored in the warehouse or carried by Units; livestock placed on the board and crops still growing were excluded. For each product, we estimated both the total quantity across the warehouse and all Units and the quantity carried by all Units.

The estimator used the following information:

- Changes to the board revealed harvesting, collection, feeding, fertilizing, and planting. We separated overnight growth and production from work performed by Units.
- We subtracted town consumption and our own trades from changes in market inventory to estimate the opponent's net purchases and sales.
- To infer fixed-price purchases of seeds and livestock, we first adjusted the opponent's bank-balance change to account for publicly visible spending and estimated trades. We then searched for purchase combinations consistent with the unexplained amount. If multiple combinations fit, we added the minimum possible quantity of each product across those combinations to its inventory estimate.

The resulting 50-dimensional feature vector was:

| Feature | Dimensions | Meaning |
|---|---:|---|
| Estimated total inventory | 12 | Quantity of each product across the warehouse and all Units |
| Estimated carried inventory | 12 | Quantity of each product carried by all Units |
| Total inventory uncertainty | 12 | Indicators derived from differences between possible purchase quantities and unexplained inventory changes |
| Carried inventory uncertainty | 12 | Total inventory uncertainty plus discrepancies between total and location-specific estimates |
| Spending with unresolved purchases | 1 | Amount not explained by fixed-price purchases, or consistent with multiple purchase combinations |
| Ambiguity in sales at the price floor | 1 | A quantity indicator derived from estimated inventory of products priced at 1 and unexplained income |

We evaluated the estimator on 275 game–player pairs from 20 submissions, covering 197,725 transitions.

| Estimate | MAE across all products and timesteps | MAE when the true quantity was nonzero | Exact quantity match rate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Total inventory | 0.702 units | 1.926 units | 86.80% |
| Carried inventory | 0.700 units | 2.799 units | 88.07% |

Only 13.2% of product–timestep pairs had nonzero total inventory, so always predicting zero would also achieve an exact match rate of 86.8% for total inventory. MAE on nonzero quantities was therefore necessary to assess the estimator's accuracy.

## Action Corrections and Final Submissions

Even after continued training, the agent still lost products to warehouse overflow and finished games with unsold inventory. Parallel Unit actions also produced conflicts over cells and seed inventory. Near the deadline, we introduced rules to correct these behaviors.

### Correcting Inventory Management and Action Conflicts

We implemented or evaluated the following eight corrections:

| ID | Correction | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| A | Sell inventory that would overflow the warehouse at night | Reduce losses from exceeding warehouse capacity |
| B | Sell warehouse inventory during the final two turns, and make loaded Units adjacent to the warehouse unload on the last turn | Avoid finishing with inventory that does not contribute to the final bank balance |
| C | Resolve duplicate actions on the same cell | Reduce wasted actions when multiple Units attempt the same task |
| D | Remove planting actions that exceed the available seed inventory | Resolve inventory conflicts between actions selected in parallel |
| E | Remove ineffective fertilizing actions | Reduce wasted actions |
| F | Remove ineffective livestock care actions | Reduce wasted actions |
| G | Remove moves outside the board | Reduce invalid actions |
| H | Switch to the heuristic solver for the final day | Use search to plan the collection and sale of remaining products |

This work began after we noticed that a PPO model under continued training was discarding substantial quantities of crops every night and retaining inventory at the end of games. Inspecting what the agent actually did revealed opportunities for improvement beyond model size and training volume.

The benefit of correction H depended on the NN's strength. For the checkpoint available one day before the deadline, using the NN through day 28 and switching to the solver on day 29 (with days indexed from zero) improved performance. We used this configuration for Final A. After one more day of NN training, however, switching to the solver reduced performance.

### Incorporating the Correction Rules into Training

We selected two approximately 10M models for our final submissions:

| Submission | Configuration | Observed result |
|---|---|---|
| Final A | Apply corrections A–G to the NN's outputs at inference time, then switch to the solver on day 29 (correction H) | Beat the same model without corrections |
| Final B | Incorporate the rules into action selection during further PPO training | Outperformed the version with inference-time corrections only |

For Final B, we incorporated corrections A–G as follows:

- **E, F, G: Invalid or ineffective actions.** Before action selection, we masked out the relevant candidates and renormalized the remaining probabilities to sum to one. PPO used the probability of the selected action under this masked distribution.
- **C, D: Duplicate tasks and seed conflicts.** Although the model computed logits for all Units in parallel, we sampled Unit actions sequentially, starting with the farmer and then the workers. For each later Unit, we masked candidates that conflicted with previously selected tasks or required more seeds than remained. This differed from inference-time correction C, which assigned a duplicated task to the Unit with the highest probability for that action; during training, the earlier Unit in the processing order kept the task.
- **A, B: Warehouse sales and end-of-game handling.** After the NN selected its actions, rules added sell orders or increased their quantities. Correction B also canceled orders involving spending, such as purchases. On the final turn, it replaced the actions of loaded Units adjacent to the warehouse with DROP. We excluded overridden action factors from both the old and new log-probabilities used by PPO.

The race with the runner-up, Victor, remained close until the end. We believe the action corrections and additional RL with those rules helped us improve the agent quickly near the end of the competition. Final A had a losing record against Victor B, as shown below, so we likely would have finished 2nd if we had submitted only Final A.

The table shows head-to-head win rates and win–loss records on the public leaderboard through the end of the match period, October 15, 2026 at 00:07 UTC. Victor A was submission 56710008, and Victor B was the final submission 56722220.

| | Victor A (56710008) | Victor B (56722220) |
|---|---:|---:|
| **Final A** (56701470) | 81.0% (136–32) | 41.9% (223–309) |
| **Final B** (56711476) | 90.7% (39–4) | **61.1% (386–246)** |

PPO training of the base model behind Final A had plateaued. Increasing model capacity was an option, but we had only one day left before submission. We chose additional RL with the rules incorporated as action masks because we expected it to produce useful gains quickly, even if the potential improvement was limited.

## Scaling the Model

When self-play plateaued, we explored changes to training settings, training volume, and model capacity. Larger models also required more computation per game, so we had to weigh capacity against the number of games we could train on in the remaining time.

### Expanding the Model While Preserving the Policy

To avoid starting over, we expanded trained Transformers using a [Net2Net](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.05641)-style approach: increasing depth or width while preserving the network's outputs. After expanding from 6 to 12 layers, we tried expanding the 12-layer, approximately 10M model to 24 layers and approximately 20M parameters. We also tested wider FFNs and models with approximately 30M parameters.

In newly added residual layers, we initialized the output projections of both attention and the FFN to zero. For a layer of the form $x+F(x)$, this makes $F(x)=0$ initially, preserving the output when the layer is inserted. The internal weights were randomly initialized so that the new layers could begin contributing as training progressed. When widening an FFN, we similarly zero-initialized the outgoing weights of the new units.

We transferred Adam's first and second moment estimates along with the existing weights, initializing optimizer state to zero only for newly added weights. For the first expansion to 20M, we also retained the KL reference policy. Before resuming PPO, we compared outputs and actions on identical observations before and after expansion, including checks for rounding differences from low-precision computation on the actual GPUs.

Preserving outputs did not guarantee further improvement. Some early expanded models became less competitive against the original model as PPO continued. Retuning parameters such as the learning rate and adding further BC eventually helped, but the teacher data and training settings also changed, so we could not isolate the effect of parameter count alone.

We submitted an approximately 30M model and confirmed that it could complete games, although its initial CPU weight loading and compilation left less runtime margin than the 20M model. We ultimately selected the 10M model lineage for our final submissions. Our experience highlighted the need to consider both the training setup after expansion and the submission runtime.

### Model Capacity and Training Volume

A plateau in win rate alone did not tell us whether the bottleneck was exploration, PPO settings, or model capacity. It helped to explore training adjustments and model expansion in parallel, carrying stronger models forward into subsequent experiments.

An open question is how the number of self-play games needed to reach a performance plateau scales with model size. Investigating this would require comparing strength as a function of game count across several model sizes while keeping the initial policies, opponents, and PPO settings matched.

## Heuristic Solver

Our heuristic solver used no learned weights. Each morning, it searched for a production plan and worker routes covering the day's 24 turns.

In Kaggriculture, crops and livestock can take several days to yield a return, so optimizing only today's profit would be shortsighted. We used Large Neighborhood Search (LNS) to optimize each day's plan, scoring candidates by the projected net cash-flow difference between the players through the final day. Exhaustively planning every worker's actions on all future days for every candidate would have been too expensive. Instead, we precomputed future actions independently for each cell using dynamic programming (DP) and used those results to evaluate plans efficiently.

![](https://www.googleapis.com/download/storage/v1/b/kaggle-user-content/o/inbox%2F4817149%2F1ef9d4d428247a280f0678b6fda34a47%2Fheuristic_overview.svg?generation=1793114936863058&alt=media)

### Searching for the Daily Plan with LNS

For each worker, a plan specified an ordered list of cells to visit and a sequence of actions to perform at each cell. Each cell had several candidate action sequences. For a crop cell, these might include WATER, WATER followed by HARVEST, or HARVEST followed by PLANT. The plan also specified the number of hired workers and whether to buy more land.

![](https://www.googleapis.com/download/storage/v1/b/kaggle-user-content/o/inbox%2F4817149%2F725f0f38aa3138ab6756e1e048c2a5e2%2Fheuristic_plan.svg?generation=1793114948105069&alt=media)

We built initial solutions under several combinations of worker count and land expansion. Candidate tasks at each cell were prioritized by value and required work, then inserted into feasible routes one at a time.

LNS repeatedly removed a randomly chosen fraction of tasks from the plan and reinserted them within a simulated annealing framework. Reinsertion could change the assigned worker, visit order, and action sequence, as well as the number of workers and the land expansion decision. Insertion decisions considered a task's future value, additional travel time, required action count, and cash and time constraints.

Submissions from September 8–12 reached a public score of approximately 2,990 and took 1st place at the time. By weighing land costs against future returns after expansion, LNS naturally selected repeated BUY_LAND actions and used all four plots. Few teams used the fourth plot then, making this a distinctive feature of the solver.

### Evaluating Plans Through the Final Day

LNS compared plans using:

`Score = today's net cash flow difference + discounted sum of projected net cash flow differences on future days`

The difference was our net cash flow minus the opponent's, with future contributions discounted by day. The current day's calculation included purchases of seeds, livestock, wheat, and fertilizer, as well as hiring and land costs.

We simulated all 24 turns of the current day. Worker movement, PICKUP, DROP, and farming actions determined when products arrived at the warehouse. The simulation accounted for market order sequencing affecting when workers could begin acting, warehouse capacity, and the limit of 10 orders per turn. It also predicted the opponent's simultaneous trades and updated prices as it calculated trading income and expenses.

For later days, we used a daily approximation rather than searching for worker routes. For each cell, the DP described below provided a future action sequence from which we estimated daily production, consumption, and workload. We aggregated these estimates across the farm by day and product to project net cash flow, including market price changes and hiring costs. We approximated future worker counts from the projected workload.

We did not optimize the opponent's future actions. Instead, we applied simple rules to their publicly visible farm: continue watering and harvesting crops; feed and care for livestock and collect their products; and replant wheat and carrots after harvesting. This produced estimates of the opponent's purchases and sales by day and product. Hidden opponent inventory was estimated from past changes in the farm, market, and cash balance.

### Precomputing Future Actions with Per-Cell DP

To make future projections tractable, we assumed that a cell's actions on later days depended only on the action sequence chosen for that cell today. Before running LNS, we used DP to precompute a future action sequence for each candidate choice at each cell. When LNS changed today's choice for a cell, we looked up the corresponding precomputed sequence instead of running DP again.

We solved the DP independently for each cell. Its state included the crop or livestock type, growth stage, harvestable quantity, watering or feeding status, fertilizer effects, and other properties of that cell. It excluded other cells and worker positions. Let $V(d,s)$ be the value obtainable through the final day from a cell in state $s$ on day $d$:

$$
V(d,s)=\max_a\{r(a)+\gamma V(d+1,s')\}.
$$

Here, `a` is the day's action sequence, `s'` is the next day's state, and `γ` is the discount factor. The daily return `r(a)` is sales revenue at forecast prices minus material costs and an approximate labor cost. Instead of computing routes, we made the labor cost proportional to the number of actions and cell visits.

We forecast prices from projected market inventory. Inventory changes were based on town shop demand and the production of both players' existing crops and livestock under rules similar to those used for the opponent forecast. For shops that had not yet appeared, we averaged over multiple scenarios.

Starting from the state after today's candidate action sequence, we followed the actions that maximized $V$ on each subsequent day. This gave the future action sequence associated with that candidate.

For every candidate at every cell, we precomputed future daily production, consumption, and workload from the associated sequence. To evaluate a plan, we summed these quantities across the selected candidates to obtain the farm's future production and requirements. When LNS changed a cell's actions, it only needed to substitute the precomputed quantities; there was no need to rerun DP during search.

![](https://www.googleapis.com/download/storage/v1/b/kaggle-user-content/o/inbox%2F4817149%2F1685fbb3a15dab3455358243a81f1d5c%2Fheuristic_future_projection.svg?generation=1793114963492061&alt=media)

The market simulation used these aggregate production quantities. Even if melons looked profitable in each cell's independent DP, selling a large volume across the farm could depress their price. Simply summing DP values would miss this effect. We therefore used DP values only for candidate generation and prioritization; complete plans were compared using the net cash flow difference from the farm-wide market simulation.

### Development and Role in the Final Solution

We started developing the solver in early September, with the first implementation written by ChatGPT (GPT-6 Pro). Subsequent gains came mainly from improving the evaluation function and making the solver faster. Submissions from September 12–15 reached public scores around 3,150 and took 1st place at the time.

The PPO-trained NN eventually became stronger. The solver then served as a source of additional BC demonstrations for situations the NN struggled with, such as sharp price increases for particular products.

For Final A, we replaced the NN with the solver on the last day (day 29, using zero-based indexing; see [Action Corrections and Final Submissions](#action-corrections-and-final-submissions)). A persistent limitation of the solver was the difficulty of forecasting future outcomes, especially the opponent's behavior. On the final day, that limitation largely disappeared because no later days remained. Collecting the remaining crops and transporting them to the warehouse also resembled a vehicle routing problem (VRP), making it well suited to search.

### Letting an NN Make the Daily Plan

We also tried a hybrid in which an NN decided only the number of cells allocated to each crop and the worker count each morning, leaving placement and action execution to a heuristic. We hoped that reducing 720 turns to 30 daily decisions would make RL easier, but this approach did not match our other models.

We did not experimentally isolate the causes. Possible explanations include slower game collection due to the CPU executor, difficulty for the NN in anticipating how the workers would execute its plans, and the lack of direct control over sale timing.

### Submission Strategy with Public Replays

Public replays were valuable training data, but submitting a strong model also exposed its behavior for other teams to imitate. We observed cases in which a policy trained on the leader's replays outperformed a PPO-trained policy that had reached roughly 30th place. Discovering a strategy through exploration could require far more computation than learning it from demonstrations.

Only a team's two most recent submissions continued playing and generating public replays. After confirming that a candidate could reach 1st place, we therefore submitted two weaker agents to shorten the period during which the stronger policy generated replays. We kept our strongest candidates back until close to the deadline, leaving somewhat weaker agents active on the public leaderboard. We considered this necessary to limit the opportunity for teams with more compute to learn from our replays and readily produce stronger agents.

A day or two after we released one of our final candidates ahead of the deadline, submissions appeared with very similar openings, farm layouts, and warehouse management. Our candidate had a losing record against them. This was an observation based on replay similarities; we did not directly verify how those teams trained their agents. We saw no comparable signs for the other candidate, which we submitted shortly before the deadline and which held its position through the end.

## How the Public Meta Evolved

Over the competition, leading strategies shifted from fixed action sequences toward policies that adapted to the game state.

| Period | Changes observed in replays |
|---|---|
| August | Fixed action scripts, or "tapes," that followed the same sequence regardless of the opponent were common. By late August, a public notebook based on then-leader Milan Leonard's strategy had also become widespread. |
| Early September | Hybrid agents appeared: they followed a fixed opening through the first melon harvest, then adapted farm management to shops and prices. |
| Mid-September onward | Agents that adapted through NNs or daily search increasingly occupied the top ranks. |

Among the agents we examined, Crop Dusta stood out as an adaptive policy in late August. Hybrid agents such as keiz later began winning those matchups. When we classified the 25 teams in the gold medal range based on daily changes in their boards, the number of adaptive agents rose from 7 on September 6 to 16 on September 12, and to almost all 25 by September 24.

Per-turn runtime offered additional clues. An agent that spent much longer on the first turn each morning might be performing daily search, while one that spent ten to twenty seconds at the start of a game might be loading or compiling an NN. These were inferences from replays, which we used to inform our choice of teachers and evaluation opponents.

These observations informed both our training data and action corrections. In late September, we added replays from policies that used the fourth plot, including replays from DSM, to the imitation-learning data for the final model lineage. Analysis of sales at very low prices and warehouse management also informed our selling rules.
